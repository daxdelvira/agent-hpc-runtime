\section{Conclusion}

Agentic steering breaks the phase predictability that byte-oriented staging
depends on, and the systems built on it fail structurally rather than for want
of accuracy. The unit of work is wrong as well: for these workloads most of
the cost of making data usable is a representation change performed inside a
live process, and that structure and a parked model's weights are the same
kind of object---anonymous memory drawn from one allocation. \sysname{}
manages them together, ranking residency by stall avoided per gigabyte held
and bounding speculation to memory that retention has not claimed.

The result is bounded in ways we report rather than avoid. Retention cannot
help a first use, so its advantage fades as the resource population grows;
staging cannot help without a window, so it fades as inter-step gaps shrink;
and above roughly 86\% of the total footprint no eviction occurs at all and
the arbitration problem disappears, though staging continues to pay there. The
policy results are trace-driven simulation over constants measured on
hardware, and an end-to-end measurement of the full system is the single
outstanding piece of evidence. The CPU-interference bound is established only
for under-subscribed cores at small working sets, and memory-bandwidth
contention at the scale of a fully activated artifact remains untested.
